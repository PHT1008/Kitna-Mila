What Did We Make?

The money multiple (MOIC) tells you how many times the cheque came back. IRR tells you how fast. A good deal needs both.

Where Did It Come From?

Profit from higher revenue and better margins is earned. Profit from a higher exit multiple is partly the market's mood. The bridge separates the two.

How the Numbers Are Worked Out

Plain-English versions of every formula in the simulator.

Pricing the deal. The company's value (enterprise value, EV) is EBITDA times the entry multiple. Take away net debt and you get the value of the shares (equity).

EV = EBITDA × multiple    Equity = EV − net debt

Secondary or primary. In a secondary deal the fund buys existing shares, so the cheque goes to the sellers. In a primary deal the cheque goes into the company, so the company's cash goes up and the fund's stake is of the bigger, post-money company.

Primary cheque = stake × pre-money equity ÷ (1 − stake)

Each year. Revenue grows at the growth rate. Margin moves in a straight line to the exit target. Free cash flow then pays down debt.

FCF = EBITDA − interest − tax − capex − increase in working capital

Exit. The buyer pays exit-year EBITDA times the exit multiple. Sale costs and net debt come off, and the fund gets its stake of what is left.

Proceeds = stake × (EV at exit − net debt at exit − sale costs)

Returns. MOIC is everything the fund received (exits and dividends) divided by everything it paid in. IRR is worked out on actual dates, the same way Excel's XIRR does it, so staged cheques, dividends and partial exits are timed correctly.

IRR = the rate where the value today of every dated cash flow adds up to zero (days ÷ 365)

Partial exits. Each exit sells a share of the fund's stake at the company's value on that date, worked out between year ends in a straight line. An IPO usually means a first sale in the offer and the rest after the lock-up ends, often at a different price.

P/E exits. Listed companies are usually priced on profit after tax. Equity value is profit after tax times the P/E, because interest is already taken off profit. The bridge converts this back to an EV multiple so it stays comparable with entry.

Equity value = PAT × P/E    Implied EV = equity value + net debt

Dividends. Once the company has no net debt, the share of free cash flow you set is paid out each year to all shareholders. The fund receives its share, in proportion to the shares it holds that year.

The Excel model. Download Excel gives a working model with live formulas: change any value in column B of the Inputs sheet, a growth or margin rate on Projections, or a cheque or exit date on Returns, and everything recalculates, including XIRR. A check row on the Returns sheet confirms the bridge adds up to zero. Sensitivity grids and scenarios are saved as values because Excel data tables can't be created from a browser.

The bridge. The fund's profit splits exactly into five parts: more revenue at the old margin and multiple, the margin change on exit-year revenue, the multiple change on exit-year EBITDA, the change in net debt, and costs.

Price check. The simulator searches for the highest entry multiple, the lowest exit multiple and the lowest growth rate that still give your target IRR. It ignores downside protection, so the business has to earn the return on its own.

Downside protection. A 1x liquidation preference means the fund gets its cheque back first (up to what the equity is worth) before others share in a sale. A promoter buyback floor means the promoter must buy the fund out at a price that gives a minimum IRR. For a foreign fund, FDI rules do not allow an assured exit price, so the simulator ignores the floor and shows a warning. A floor is also only as good as the promoter's ability to pay.

USD returns. A fund that reports in dollars loses the rupee's fall each year.

USD IRR = (1 + rupee IRR) ÷ (1 + rupee fall a year) − 1

Tax on LPs. Indian AIFs in Category I and II pass gains through to their investors. Long-term gains on unlisted shares held over 24 months are taxed at 12.5% without indexation, a rate Budget 2026 left unchanged. Shares held 24 months or less give short-term gains, taxed at slab rates.

Debt in Indian buyouts. From 2026, RBI allows banks to fund acquisitions within limits, but the framework excludes financial acquirers, so a PE fund's acquisition vehicle may not qualify. Much acquisition debt still comes from non-bank lenders, so use an interest rate that matches the lender you expect.

Assumptions

All tranches buy shares at the entry price. Interest is charged on opening net debt, so a tranche that reaches the company mid-year earns interest from the next year. Values between year ends are worked out in a straight line. Tax is charged on profit after depreciation and interest, and never goes below zero; tax losses are not carried forward. The management incentive pool comes out of exit equity before the fund's share. The liquidation preference is non-participating and, like the buyback floor, applies only to a single full exit. Carry is taken from the last distribution and capped so LPs still earn the hurdle. Gross returns are before any tax; the Gross to Net tab applies your tax rate to LPs' gains.

Learn the Concepts

A five-minute crash course in plain English. These examples use simple round numbers and are not linked to the simulator, so they always stay the same.

First things first

What Does a PE Fund Do?

A private equity fund collects money from investors (LPs), buys stakes in private companies, works with management for three to seven years, and then sells. Its whole job comes down to one question: how much more will someone pay for this company later than we pay for it today?

In India, many PE deals are minority stakes in promoter-run companies, often with little or no new debt. Others are full buyouts funded partly with debt. The simulator handles both.

The three levers

Only Three Things Make Money

1

EBITDA growth. Sell more, or keep more of every rupee sold.

2

Multiple change. The next buyer pays more for each rupee of EBITDA.

3

Debt paydown. Cash from the business repays the loan, so more of the company's value belongs to the shareholders.

Borrowing means the fund puts in less of its own money, so every rupee of value gained or lost hits the fund's equity harder.

Simple exampleA ₹100 cr company sells for ₹130 cr. Without debt the fund makes 1.3x. With ₹60 cr of debt it put in ₹40 cr and gets ₹70 cr: 1.75x. If it sells for ₹70 cr instead, it is 0.7x versus 0.25x.
The Value Bridge, Worked Through
Buy at ₹10 cr EBITDA and 10x with ₹40 cr of debt: equity is ₹60 cr. Sell at ₹15 cr EBITDA and 12x with ₹20 cr of debt: equity is ₹160 cr. The ₹100 cr gain splits into ₹50 cr from EBITDA growth (₹5 cr more EBITDA at the old 10x), ₹30 cr from the multiple (2 more turns on ₹15 cr), and ₹20 cr from debt paid down.
Gross Returns and Net Returns
Fund investors usually pay a yearly management fee (often around 2%) and give the manager a share of profits called carry (often 20%) once a minimum return called the hurdle (often 8% a year) is met. Example: a deal turns ₹100 cr into ₹250 cr in 5 years, which is 2.5x and about 20.1% a year gross. Five years of fees add ₹10 cr, profit is ₹140 cr, and carry takes ₹28 cr. Investors get ₹222 cr on ₹110 cr paid in: about 2.0x and 15.6% a year net.
Hurdle and Catch-Up
The hurdle means the manager earns no carry until investors have their money back plus the hurdle return. After that, a catch-up usually lets the manager take most of the next profits until it has its full share, for example 20% of all profit. The Gross to Net tab uses a full catch-up.
Primary and Secondary Money
If the fund buys shares from the promoter, the promoter gets the cash and the company is unchanged. If the fund subscribes to new shares, the company gets the cash to spend on growth, and the fund's stake is of a bigger company. Many Indian growth deals mix both.
Why a High IRR Can Mislead
A deal that returns 1.3x in one year shows a 30% IRR, but it may have made far less money than a 3x deal over five years. Funds look at both, along with the actual rupees made, before calling a deal a success.
DPI and TVPI
These are fund-level versions of MOIC. DPI is cash already paid back to investors divided by what they paid in. TVPI adds the value of companies the fund still holds. DPI is cash in hand; TVPI is partly an estimate.

Anyone deciding whether a company is worth buying ends up at the same question: what will this return, and where will that return come from? A strong headline number can hide a lot. Part of the return comes from the business growing, part from paying down debt, and part from the next buyer simply paying a higher multiple. Only the first two are really in anyone’s control. “Kitna Mila” separates them on one page, in plain numbers, so anyone can see what a deal actually depends on.
